Who this is for:

A five-person old-school long-only manager, ~$400M AUM, 25-30 concentrated positions held three to five years. Two PMs, two analysts, one operations person. Tooling is Bloomberg, Excel, and filings; there are no engineers and no data scientists, and nobody here maintains a database. They buy research and datasets occasionally but cannot operate infrastructure. What kills them is being wrong about a business, not transaction costs. Anything proposed must be executable by one analyst and defensible to an investment committee that is skeptical of models it cannot inspect.

Research question:

A company just guided to product shortages lasting through next year. Does that portend more customer long-term agreements and more durable revenue?

---

PLAN A:

Management says supply is short through next year and customers are locking in volume via long-term agreements (LTAs), read by the market as a duration story deserving a higher multiple. Two claims must hold: customers sign binding commitments, and those commitments survive normalized supply.

Hypothesis: shortage-era LTAs are honored in volume and broken in price — customer capacity insurance and supplier retention tool, not a revenue floor. Outcomes are bimodal, split by switching cost, not contract language. Where in-term requalification is impossible (aerospace forgings, auto power semis mid-program, qualified HBM stacks), agreements hold near stated terms. Where requalification is cheap or fungible (polysilicon, mature-node logic, container slots), agreements reprice to spot within two to four quarters of overhang landing, since suppliers won't sue their biggest customers. Secondary hypothesis: a large disclosed LTA book is a coincident indicator of cycle peak, not durability — prepayments funding the book fund the capacity that destroys the pricing. Falsifier: episodes where contracted price stayed materially above spot for 8+ quarters post-normalization, absent a hard qualification moat.

Episodes (20-30, cross-industry, matched to customer disclosure): polysilicon 2007-2012 (Hemlock, Wacker, OCI, MEMC, REC — $60-100/kg take-or-pay against spot spiking to $400 then below $25; renegotiation, walks, litigation incl. Hemlock v. SolarWorld); auto/industrial semis 2021-2024 (Infineon, NXP, STMicro, onsemi ~$16bn committed LTSA volume, Renesas's ~$2bn deposit to Wolfspeed) through the 2023-2025 correction; memory twice — DRAM/NAND 2017-2019 (completed cycle) and HBM 2023-2026 (live holdout); MLCC/passives 2017-2019 (Murata, Samsung Electro-Mechanics — worst case); ABF substrates 2021-2023 (Ibiden, Unimicron, Nan Ya); uranium 2007-2013 (flex-down, market-related pricing with floors); LNG SPAs 2008/2012 (highest-quality LTAs, reopened via price-review clauses — upper bound); ocean containers 2021-2023 ($6-8k/FEU abandoned within months, no enforcement); lithium/cobalt offtakes 2017-2020, 2022-2024 (Livent/Tesla, SQM/Albemarle); titanium/aero forgings (best case: hold, showing label isn't the variable).

Data: supplier 10-Ks/20-Fs, Exhibit 10 contracts, ASC 606 RPO/contract-liability disclosures; customer purchase-obligation tables (Item 7); price series (TrendForce, Fastmarkets/Benchmark, UxC, Argus/ICIS, Drewry/Xeneta); customs/bill-of-lading data (Panjiva, ImportGenius); legal filings (PACER, Delaware Chancery, ICC/SIAC, foreign courts); capacity data (SEMI fab database, capex guidance, qualification lags by end market).

Method: (1) define event at first allocation-language filing, T=0, via EDGAR search, kept regardless of outcome to avoid hindsight bias; (2) code contracts — take-or-pay vs. reservation, make-up rights, prepayment refundability, pricing, MFN clauses, flex bands, reopeners, termination penalties, counterparty credit; (3) code outcomes on an ordinal scale at T+4/8/12 quarters, separating price from volume; (4) compute unconditional and conditional base rates by feature (qualification lag, sourcing, credit, pricing), ranked not regressed; (5) build a capacity ledger per market (additions, ramp curves, hidden capacity) for the gap between supply-demand balance and weighted-average LTA maturity — the key predictor; (6) customer breach arithmetic — spot savings vs. penalty PV, assuming suppliers preemptively concede.

Validation: hold out the 2021-2024 auto-semi cycle, build the framework on the rest, predict holdout outcomes with dates before checking. For the live HBM case, pre-register 3-5 falsifiable checks (contract-liability direction, RPO growth threshold, customs shipment tracking, customer 10-K obligation-table increase), each checkable within 12 months. Reconcile supplier, customer, and shipment data; where they disagree, shipment data wins. Test that aerospace/LNG score durable and MLCC/containers fragile, ordered by qualification lag not label.

Pitfalls: definitional drift across LTA types, exploited by issuers; disclosure asymmetry (signature announced, unwind silent) biasing toward durability; double-counting backlog/RPO/LTA value; prepayment as disguised financing funding the capacity ending the shortage; index pass-through masking real price cuts; conflating demand-driven shortage-in-growth with supply-shock-in-flat-demand (likely the dominant variance driver); survivorship bias as failed suppliers vanish from the record. Output: a probability-weighted haircut, not a verdict, with a statement of what would justify the higher multiple.

---

PLAN B:

Hypothesis (falsifiable): shortage guidance causes a step-up in committed forward volume that converts to revenue at a rate, and over a horizon, sufficient to materially reduce forward revenue variance versus history — and current EV does not already price that. Prior: the lock-in link is real; enforceable, cash-backed commitment is usually weak. The model separates the two and prices the residue.

Data required: quarterly RPO (ASC 606-10-50-13 footnote, 12-month + remainder); Item 101 "backlog" (company-defined, no accounting standard); announced LTA values (8-Ks, IR decks, prepared remarks, customer press releases, commitments footnote); ten-percent-customer/risk-factor disclosures; customers' purchase-obligation tables; balance-sheet contract liabilities/deposits, cash flow; peer and prior-cycle backlog/LTA/realized-revenue history.

Methodology: build a quarterly forward revenue bridge (8 quarters past guided resolution) tagging each dollar by strength of claim. Roll forward RPO (start + gross bookings − revenue recognized − cancellations ± FX/M&A), solving net bookings as a plug and backing into cancellations from book-to-bill; negative implied gross bookings signals hidden cancellations. Keep RPO, backlog, and LTA value separate — the LTA-to-RPO gap is the key signal. Agreement register (counterparty, value, term, ramp, product line), scored on six attributes: minimum quantities vs. best-efforts; fixed vs. indexed/renegotiable pricing; customer cash (prepayment, reservation fee, capex funding, visible on balance sheet/cash flow); take-or-pay damages percentage; non-cancelable vs. reschedulable within a lead-time window; RPO inclusion. Tier/haircut: Tier 1 (cash, real take-or-pay, in RPO) ~90–95%; Tier 2 (binding minimums, in RPO, no cash) ~70%; Tier 3 (MOUs/partnerships, outside RPO) 25–35%, near zero in downturn — calibrated from the prior episode's (2021–23 semiconductors) realized backlog conversion, using worse company history where it exists. Coverage = haircut-adjusted committed revenue ÷ modeled revenue per quarter, annualized per segment (not company-wide/single-year), split into quantity and price coverage. Segment by product line (bottleneck vs. discretionary capacity) and customer (concentration index, correlated-failure risk). Cross-check via customer mirror disclosures — recovery <~50% implies non-binding commitments. Three resolution scenarios (2Q early, on guidance, 4Q late), full paths: early brings pricing rolloff, pushout exercise, inventory air-pocket; late extends pricing but invites second-sourcing. Carry capex into a 10–15-year depreciation schedule against 3-year agreements — margin-trough risk. Compare EV/haircut-adjusted committed gross profit (normalized margin) across time and peers, plus a reverse DCF for market-implied vs. modeled durable horizon.

Validation: backtest shortage-guidance episodes (memory/passives 2017–18; semiconductors 2021–22; AI-cycle transformers/turbines/HBM; telecom 2000) for agreement counts/values, RPO trajectory, realized vs. implied coverage, and forward returns, to isolate what distinguished durable cases. Coherence checks: backlog rising with finished-goods inventory; DSO lengthening during a claimed shortage; reservation/cancellation fees booked as revenue but not demand — stripped from coverage math. Read transcripts adversarially for term drift (non-cancelable, take-or-pay, prepayment, sold out); reconstruct on one definition, footnoting restatements.

Pitfalls: double-ordering across suppliers inflates backlog beyond plausible demand (check cross-supplier sum vs. independent unit forecasts); enforcement — suppliers rarely sue major customers, so damages are an upper bound, renegotiation the likely outcome absent cash received; reflexivity — shortage guidance induces the agreement-signing being measured; announcement count carries no information, only structural quality and conversion; duration/level confusion — flat-volume long-dated coverage is durability without growth, a downgrade against a growth multiple.

Thresholds — constructive requires all of: ≥60% NTM revenue in Tier 1/2 with a stated ship schedule; ≥40% year-two coverage; cash support ≥15% of committed value or take-or-pay damages >20% of shortfall; top-3 customer share <45%; mirror disclosures reconciling to ≥50%; modeled horizon exceeding market-implied by ≥2 years. Negative on any of: negative implied gross bookings two straight quarters; channel inventory >1.5x normal; a backlog/LTA definition change in a quarter that would otherwise have declined; supplier finished-goods inventory rising two quarters while backlog is called record.
