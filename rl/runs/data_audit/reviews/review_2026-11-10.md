Checked and found sound:

- **Gold correctness.** Each injector records the window it changed. Ticker-wide natural items map through the same
  disguise as the data.
- **Point-in-time safety.** An audit is retrospective, so the full history is allowed and the gold uses no outside
  data. The one look-ahead risk is downstream, in how fixes are used (W6).
- **Grader edges.** Duplicate reports of one matched problem are neither credited nor penalised. Low-volume and
  optional series are ignored. The recorded attacks (empty, random, extreme, injection, tamper) all score 0.
- **Ambiguous types.** `double_count` and a 1.9x `level_shift` are interchangeable on purpose, and the data can't
  tell them apart.

## Added 2026-11-10: per-series usability verdict (feedback log #12-#16)

Built 2026-11-10, uncommitted (feedback log #17). Bench: the reference scores 0.938 on average with every verdict right; naive 0.075. Spec:

- **Deliverable:** a usable / not-usable verdict for every (ticker, source) series, after the fixes the audit
  proposes, with a one-line reason.
- **Unrecoverable:** an outage (missing or near-dark days) of more than 7 consecutive days. Shorter gaps are filled
  from the seasonal pattern, and every other problem type has an exact or peer-based fix. The 7-day cut is a
  proposal; Mayank's example puts 2 days in 100 on the recoverable side and a missing recent month on the other.
- **Not usable** if an unrecoverable period touches the last 90 days of the panel, or the series has 3 or more
  unrecoverable periods, or they cover more than 10% of its history. Low-volume series (median under 10 a day)
  are not scored, as in `issues.json`.
- **Generator:** two new placements, no new issue types: an outage that runs to the end of the panel, and a series
  with repeated outages. Both are reported in `issues.json` as `outage` items.
- **Reward:** 0.7 × issues F1 + 0.3 × F1 on the not-usable class, then scaled by the write-up (D3).

## Proposed 2026-11-10: new problems, graded repairs, harder rubric (feedback log #24-#27)

### 1. Daylight-saving date offset (new type `dst_shift`)

**The mechanism.** The vendor cuts each source's daily batch at a fixed 04:30 UTC. It stamps the batch with the
local (Eastern) date at the moment of the cut.
- In winter, 04:30 UTC is 23:30 local, so the stamp is the day just ending: correct.
- From the spring clock change, 04:30 UTC is 00:30 local the next morning, so every day's spend carries the
  next day's date until clocks fall back.
- On the spring change one date has no batch and the next carries a day's spend. In autumn one date carries
  two days.

The mirror case, a cut just before local midnight in summer, gives the one-day-early version you described:
Tuesday's spend appears on Monday. The generator plants either direction.

**How it is planted.** On one source, every year from some year onward, between the US clock-change dates (second
Sunday of March, first Sunday of November) on the disguised calendar. The other sources are untouched.
- Detection: the source's weekday pattern sits one day off its peers in summer and lines up in winter.
- Repair: re-date the summer rows; split the doubled day using the source's weekday pattern; impute the missing
  date.
- Gold: one item per summer window. A report can cover the recurring pattern as one item with `"recurring": true`.

### 2. Temporary x100 (`unit_scale` with an end)

Since #23, half of the `unit_scale` plants are already temporary (14-90 days): `amt` jumps 100x while `ct` and
the trend are unchanged, then returns. Proposal: make every plant temporary and stretch the window to 14-180 days.

### 3. Grading the repair, not just the flag

**The task asks for a fourth file, `output/cleaned.csv`.** It is the panel with every problem fixed:
- recover as much of the real signal as possible (deduplicate, swap back, rescale, re-date, impute short gaps);
- where the signal can't be recovered, keep the row but leave `ct` and `amt` empty (null);
- leave every row with no problem exactly as it came.

**What the grader compares against.** The panel the generator holds before planting, which is the true
answer for every planted problem. It is saved hidden in `tests/`, with the gold.

**Repair score, per required planted item,** over its window on its series:

| Right handling | What it checks |
|---|---|
| fixable (duplicates, swaps, unit, date and DST shifts, series swap, double count, level shift) | share of days whose `ct` and `amt` are within 5% of the true values |
| short gap (7 days or less) | share of days imputed within 20% of the true values |
| unrecoverable (longer outage, near-dark) | share of days left null; an imputed value there counts as wrong |

**Damage.** Every other day is checked for being left alone. A good row changed by more than 1% or nulled counts
as damage. Damage multiplies the repair score by (1 - 10 x damaged share), floored at 0.
- This is the "punish touching true data" rule from #10, applied to the data itself and not only to the flags.

**Real breaks** (DDS and the airline source breaks) have no true values to compare against:
- an all-source outage should be nulled;
- the others are graded on the flag only.

**Weights, open:** proposed issues F1 0.4 + repair 0.4 + usability 0.2, still scaled by the note.

The instruction gains the rule as you worded it: recover as much of the underlying signal as you can, otherwise
mark the point or period unrecoverable and null it.

### 4. Other ways to make it harder (#27), strongest first

1. **Real events as decoys.**
   - What: a one-week promotion (x1.4-1.8) or a storm drop, planted identically in every source of a ticker.
   - Scored: they are not problems, so flagging one is a false report and "fixing" one is damage.
   - Why: this tests the core judgment directly, rather than relying on DDS alone.
2. **Stacked problems.** Two problems on one series, e.g. a date shift and later a level shift, or a double count
   inside a unit switch. Each has to be separated and repaired in the right order.
3. **Amount-only shifts.** From some date a source includes sales tax or fees: `amt` rises 6-10% and `ct` doesn't.
   Only the ticket-size check against peers catches it.
4. **A replayed batch.** One day's rows arrive again under the next date, and that next day's real rows are lost.
   - It looks like a normal day, but it duplicates the previous day's weekday pattern exactly.
   - Repair: drop the replay and impute the lost day.
5. **Short partial losses.** 1-3 days at 40-70% of volume. The right answer is to impute, not to null or ignore;
   this tests the line between short and long gaps.
6. **A downstream check.**
   - What: grade each ticker's quarterly year-on-year growth, computed from `cleaned.csv`, against the same
     figure from the true panel.
   - Why: it is outcome-graded, in line with the pilot's strategy. It also catches repairs that look right day by
     day but bias the aggregate.
7. **More problems per task and a word cap.** 5-9 planted problems instead of 3-5, and a 120-word cap on the note.
   Also the sizes in the previous section (shifts of 1.15-1.35x, double counts of 15-50%).

**My pick:** 1-3 first. Repair grading is the biggest change in difficulty: Opus's flags are near perfect, but
nothing yet checks whether its fixes are right. Decoys and stacked problems then attack the flagging that's still
easy. 6 is cheap once `cleaned.csv` exists.

### 5. Making real events decidable in a closed environment (#28)

Every planted call has to be decidable from what is in the task folder. The data alone can settle most of them:
- A real shock moves every source, and usually several companies in the same industry, on the same days.
- A promotion raises counts while lowering the average ticket.
- A collection error moves one source, or moves all of a ticker's sources in ways no business does (near-dark,
  snapping back).
The calls the data can't settle need attached context.

**The proposal: a documentation bundle with the same form in every task, controls included.** Its presence
then says nothing about whether a given task has problems.
- **`docs/vendor_notes.md`, the vendor's dated release notes per source.** Feed migrations, a processor change
  that adds tax to amounts, a known outage with backfill pending, and routine entries that change nothing.
- **`docs/company_events.csv`, a dated event calendar per ticker.** Promotions, fare sales, weather closures,
  store openings and closures, earnings dates. Most events leave no visible mark in the data.
- Both are generated from the task seed, in disguised tickers and dates, so the environment stays deterministic.

**Rules, so the bundle can't be used as a shortcut:**
- Every entry is true, so the gold never depends on a lie.
- The bundle is incomplete. Some real errors and some real events are undocumented, and must be caught from the
  data alone.
- Many entries leave no visible mark, so "in the calendar" never means "explains this".
- **Trap:** a documented promotion in the same week as an undocumented outage on one source. The promotion must
  not excuse the outage.

**Is it worth it?** Yes. Reconciling a panel with the vendor's notes and the company's calendar is a real part of
alt-data work, and it is where judgment beats pattern-matching. The cost is a longer prompt, plus a template the
generator has to keep varied enough that it can't be regex-matched into the answer.

**Revised after #29: drop the bundle.** Mayank's objection holds. If the notes name the events, the task becomes a
lookup. If they don't, writing a realistic, complete-enough calendar is a lot of work, and the incompleteness rule
makes it a guessing game.

In practice an analyst has no per-company event log. They have the data, their knowledge of the industry, and the
public calendar. When a one-company move can't be settled from those, they ask the vendor; they don't guess.

So plant only decoys the data itself settles, and never plant one that only a document could settle:
- **Industry-wide shocks.** A 2-5 day drop of 20-50% across every source of every company in one industry, e.g.
  a storm grounding all the airlines. An error doesn't hit several companies at once.
- **Recurring company events.** A promotion spike in the same week every year, in every source. One year alone
  looks like an error; the repetition makes it real. The DDS late-February spike in the raw data is already one,
  and Opus reasoned it out.
- **The promotion signature.** Counts rise and the average ticket falls, in every source: discounting, not
  double counting, which keeps the ticket size the same.

A one-off, one-company, all-source jump with nothing else to go on stays out. The only exception is a move no
business makes, like the DDS collapse.

**Revised after #30: count sources, not companies.** A collection error can hit many companies at once: one
vendor's feed failing for everyone. So "several companies moved together" doesn't make a move real.

What identifies a collection problem is that the sources are independent. One vendor's failure shows up in that
vendor's source, whatever the number of companies it covers.

| What moves | Call | Planted as |
|---|---|---|
| some sources, one or more companies (up to every company) | collection error | **new:** one source drops or goes near-dark for every company at once (`source_outage`) |
| every source, one industry, the other industries flat | real | decoy: industry shock |
| every source of one company, plausible size and shape | real | decoys: recurring event, discount signature |
| every source of one company, implausible (near-dark, snaps back) | collection error | as now (DDS) |
| every source of every company | undecidable: macro or a broad collection failure | not planted; would be optional if it occurs naturally |

The instruction's rule changes to match: a move confined to some of the sources is a data problem, however many
companies it touches. A move in every source is real unless no business moves that way. A move in every source of
every company can't be settled from this data, so say so rather than guess.

W5 would then credit a note that names such a period as a limit.
