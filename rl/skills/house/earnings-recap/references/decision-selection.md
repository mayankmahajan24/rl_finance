# Earnings recap decision selection

Use this after the complete-call ledger. Choose the few
conclusions with the highest investment decision value; do not recreate the transcript
or force one item from every ledger category into the post.

## Build one candidate slate

Create a compact card for each serious candidate:

`new claim and comparator -> mechanism -> magnitude and duration -> strongest adverse
evidence -> estimate, thesis, action, catalyst, risk, or portfolio consequence -> proof`

Cover the current surprise and guide bridge, earnings quality, the central thesis
debate, the best leading indicator or changed long-term framework, material strategic
changes, and any first-order portfolio consequence. These are candidate pools, not
required sections. Management's denial records its view; it does not resolve a viable
alternative explanation.

## Convert facts into decisions

- Reconcile actuals with expectations and the prior outlook. For guidance, show the
  change versus the prior implied value or consensus and how much of the beat or miss
  carried forward.
- Separate acquisition, currency, price, mix, timing, accounting, and temporary items
  from organic or repeatable performance. Calculate a labeled range or per-unit bridge
  when the available inputs support one.
- When linked volume and value measures move at different rates, calculate the
  per-unit change and decide whether price, mix, or monetization strengthens or weakens
  the headline growth conclusion.
- For a changed multi-year framework, connect the endpoint to a near-term milestone,
  the required margin or cash outcome, and time to proof.
- For a strategic opportunity, distinguish recognized economics, a contracted
  commitment, pipeline, and aspiration. State how it could earn, what scale matters,
  and the next proof point.
- End each winning card with a present decision: raise, hold, or cut an estimate;
  strengthen, weaken, or reassess the thesis; or change the catalyst or risk framework.
  Make a position call only when expectations, valuation, or house context support it.
  A monitoring condition follows that decision; it does not substitute for one.

## Rank once

Rank every candidate by:

1. change versus expectations or prior underwriting;
2. importance of the decision enabled;
3. magnitude, duration, and time to proof;
4. strength of direct evidence and adverse evidence; and
5. incremental value per word after the opening is read.

Select the opening decision and its strongest counterweight, then two or three distinct
body decisions. Give extra weight to information learned on the call: a changed
framework, admission, unresolved challenge, quantified competitive mechanism,
commitment, conversion timing, or new proof point. Routine scorecard, accounting,
working-capital, and capital-allocation facts lose unless they change earnings quality,
valuation, liquidity, or the central decision.

Prefer a nearer, measurable revenue or earnings ramp supported by observed adoption,
orders, design wins, or commitments over a distant market-size claim or aspiration.
Prefer a recurring monetization mechanism over the accounting treatment of a one-time
transaction when both compete for space, unless the transaction itself changes
valuation, liquidity, or the reported guide. Preserve the weaker item as a
qualification only when it changes the winning decision.

Apply these comparisons before locking the slate:

- An explicit company-wide change in the long-term framework outranks a narrower
  market-size or product detail when it changes multi-year earnings or thesis
  underwriting; use the narrower fact as proof.
- When disclosed inputs support it, calculate a transparent range for the organic
  guide after acquisition, currency, price, mix, or timing effects. Label assumptions
  and uncertainty; do not stop at `not disclosed` when a useful bound is possible.
- Treat related products, partnerships, and regulatory changes as one business-model
  candidate when their shared value is how usage converts into revenue. Preserve the
  distinct facts only when they lead to different decisions or proof points. State
  which activity drives adoption, how it earns, and what would prove conversion.
- Derive portfolio implications from the primary demand, supply, share, pricing, or
  cost driver. Prefer the peer or category with the clearest relative exposure, and
  omit an implication that only repeats the target company's body conclusion.

Compare the strongest excluded card with the weakest selected card. Swap it in when it
changes a more important estimate, thesis, action, catalyst, risk, or portfolio view.
This is the final ranking; do not rerank through a second procedure.

Resolve the central bull/bear tradeoff in the selected slate. When valuation or house
positioning is unavailable, make the strongest supported estimate or thesis change
instead of ending with a watch list. Name the counterweight and explain which side the
current evidence favors.

## Choose implications and quotes

Rank implications separately for incremental decision value. Derive each from a
top-ranked company conclusion and compare plausible direct competitors, suppliers,
customers, and category exposures. Use:

`source fact -> economic transmission -> relative exposure -> company or category ->
sign -> proof point`

Name a company only when relative exposure supports it; otherwise use the category.
Omit `Implications` when no supported consequence beats another company decision.
Do not spend both implication slots on the target company when a top-ranked conclusion
supports a more useful consequence for a direct competitor, supplier, customer, or
category. Replace the weaker target-company repetition only after checking relative
exposure and the economic transmission.

Choose two or three exact management quotes only after the slate is fixed. Prefer a
changed framework, causal mechanism, quantified competitive intent, concession, or
adverse qualification. Preserve the speaker, wording, context, and every material
qualifier.
